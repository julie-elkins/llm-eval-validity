# Research question and claim boundary

## Primary question

Can an AI-assisted assessment workflow distinguish fluent task performance from
evidence of independent learning?

The current synthetic phase asks a narrower question:

> Can an evidence-aware rubric detect constructed cases where a correct-looking
> response is insufficient to support an inference of independent learning?

The human-learning question remains future work because this phase does not
observe real learners.

## Operational definition

For this study, **independent learning evidence** means performance on a matched
transfer task completed without AI assistance, using a task form that tests the
same target skill without repeating the original answer.

This is an operational definition—a measurable rule used for the study—not a
claim that one transfer task fully captures learning.

## Secondary questions

1. Does an evidence-aware evaluation support a better inference about independent
   transfer than final-answer-only evaluation?
2. Which evidence fields are most often missing, contradictory, or misleading?
3. How often do evaluators mistake an AI-supported correct answer for evidence of
   independent understanding?
4. Does requiring explanation, confidence, or verification expose unsupported
   performance, or merely add more fluent text?

The secondary questions are exploratory. They must not be converted into
confirmed claims if the pilot is too small or the uncertainty is wide.

## Hypotheses

### H1: Correctness is insufficient

Final-answer correctness alone will overstate the evidence for independent
learning in at least some task cases.

### H2: Evidence-aware evaluation is more discriminating

An evaluator that sees the declared evidence contract and bounded interaction
metadata will distinguish transfer-success and transfer-failure cases more
accurately than an evaluator that sees only the final answer.

### H3: Explanation length is not a validity guarantee

Longer or more fluent explanations will not be treated as evidence of learning
unless they are correct, relevant, and predictive of independent transfer.

## Claims the synthetic phase may support

- A documented comparison of answer-only and evidence-aware assessment views.
- A description of failure modes in AI-assisted assessment.
- A reproducible protocol for later human validation.
- A design recommendation for preserving evidence beyond the final answer.

## Claims the study may not support

- That AI assistance improves learning.
- That synthetic transfer dispositions are equivalent to learner outcomes.
- That the protocol measures intelligence or general ability.
- That one model, prompt, or tutor is universally better.
- That a small adult pilot generalizes to all learners or educational settings.
- That independent transfer on one task family proves durable learning.

The synthetic phase cannot establish whether a real learner transferred or
retained a skill. It can only test whether the proposed measurement catches the
failure modes it was designed to catch.

## Scope boundary

This study is about the validity of evidence used to interpret AI-assisted
assessment. It is not a private certification-item study, a production learning
platform, a psychometric instrument for individual diagnosis, or a claim about
sentience or cognitive architecture.
